Guide

The method, the risk formula, the tiers and the known limits.

How to use the desk

A stats-based filter for Premier League player-bookings markets. It ranks players by how often they get carded and give away fouls, so your per-fixture research starts in the right place. It does not pick players or price markets.

This Gameweek — the default view

The desk opens on This Gameweek: a card per fixture, each led by its referee and listing the highest-risk available players from both sides, banded by a fixture-aware booking probability. That number is the season model chance of a card (below) scaled by two match factors: the assigned referee's card rate — shown inline as the ×factor on each row and at the top of the card — and a small boost for recognised derbies. Bands are High (50%+) and Watch (30–50%); lower-risk names fold away behind a toggle. The coloured dot on each row is sample confidence from minutes played (green high, amber medium, grey low). Your starred players surface in a My watchlist card at the top, sorted by their next fixture's probability. Referee appointments aren't in the live feed, so assign the official on each card once PGMOL publishes them — every player's number then scales to his whistle. Fixtures are ordered hottest to coolest by booking heat. Every match card has a ⬇ Share match button that exports it as an image, and ⬇ Share gameweek exports the whole round's fixtures ranked by heat — both handy for FPL and tipster content. It's an estimate layered on season form, not a market price.

Player booking risk

risk = yellow cards per 90 × 2 + fouls committed per 90

Yellow rate is weighted double because the bet pays on cards, not fouls. Fouls per 90 carries the volume signal, the players who keep giving the referee a decision. Both are 2025-26 rates. Players under 450 minutes are flagged low sample.

Implied probability and value

The desk turns form into a model-implied chance of the player being carded in a match. The league base booking rate is total yellows per player-match (about —% here). The P(card) column shows the probability and its fair decimal odds (1 ÷ probability); click it to paste a bookmaker's price and see the edge, (odds × probability − 1). Positive edge means the price pays more than the model thinks the chance is worth.

The model (v2) does three things a naive score doesn't: it shrinks each player's per-90 rates toward the position average by how many minutes they've actually played (so one card in a few appearances doesn't read as an elite booker); it turns the shrunk yellow rate into a match probability through a Poisson hazard (1 − e−rate), which reproduces the division's own card rate by construction, and per fixture shifts that on the log-odds scale for the referee and derby (multiplying the odds, not the probability, so a strict ref lifts a 72% pick to ~77% rather than an implausible 94%); and it forecasts fouls directly — expected fouls and over/under lines from a Negative-Binomial count model, with a mechanistic fouls→card cross-check on each player's profile. The Model track record card scores the model out of sample (Brier, log-loss) against a base-rate baseline. Full write-up: docs/modelling-review.md. Still an estimate for research, not a market price.

Every price you type into a P(card) cell is now kept — in this browser only, and discarded after a week — and plotted on the Value view of the Players panel: the chance the price implies across, our model up. On the diagonal the two agree. The shaded band beneath it is roughly the bookmaker's margin, and a point sitting in it beats his opinion but not his cut, so it does not pay. There is no odds feed here, so the chart only ever shows prices you entered yourself, and a single price cannot have its margin removed properly — that needs both sides of the market, which nobody publishes. The band is an assumption, not a measurement.

Be honest with it: this is a season-average estimate from form data, not a market price. It knows nothing about a specific matchup beyond the risk score, referees and derbies adjust fixture heat but not this per-player number, and bookmaker odds carry margin. Use the edge as a screen for further research, never as a guarantee.

Club discipline

Clubs are tiered on cards received per game: card-heavy at 2.0 and above, middling 1.5 to 1.9, disciplined below 1.5. The flame marks a combustible side, 2.0 plus cards and 12.0 plus fouls.

Referee strictness — two signals, not one

A referee's ×factor now blends two measures. Yellows per game is the obvious one, but it's contaminated: an official shows more cards partly because the fixtures he draws are more foul-heavy. Cards per foul divides that out — how readily he reaches for the card given the same provocation. Because the desk already models each club's foul and card propensity separately, leaning on yellows per game alone double-counts the teams. The two ratios (each against the league average) are combined as a weighted geometric mean, equal weight by default, and clamped to ×0.75–×1.30 so no single official can swamp the model. Both columns are on the Referees tab; cards per foul carries its % difference from the league average. Where an official has no fouls data yet, the factor stays on yellows per game exactly as before. Fouls come from the same public football-data.co.uk match records as the card counts, so this adds no new data source.

Live data

The desk pulls the official FPL feed through its own proxy: the 2026-27 schedule for the Fixtures tab, each player's live yellow and red card count, and availability flags (injured, suspended, doubtful). Once a player has 450 minutes of 2026-27 football, his yellow rate in the risk score switches from last season's form to this season's live rate — rows carry a green dot when live rates are in use. If the feed is unreachable the desk falls back to the baked 2025-26 form and says so in the top bar. Player photos are the official cutouts from the same FPL feed; a player without one (pre-season, or unmatched) simply shows his club crest.

Team card markets — total cards and both teams carded

Each fixture card carries a market strip: EXP CARDS (the expected total in the match), O3.5 and O4.5 (the chance of 4+ and 5+ cards) and BTC (both teams carded — each side picks up at least one). These are the liquid card markets, and they fall out of the per-player numbers the desk already computes: every rated available player is one coin-flip with his own probability, so the match total follows a Poisson-binomial distribution, which has an exact answer — no simulation involved.

One correction matters. A player's percentage is his chance of a card if he plays the full 90, so simply adding up a 25-man squad would price a match with 50 players on the pitch — it returns about 9 expected cards instead of 4. Each player is therefore weighted by the share of his side's minutes he actually takes, scaled to an eleven. Across all 380 possible pairings in the shipped data the model averages 3.5 cards a match, against the 3.76 the free match records show the division actually produced (3.64 yellows plus 0.12 reds) — so it runs about 8% conservative, the same direction and size as the Championship and La Liga desks, which is what makes the three comparable on Today's Card. It prices away sides above home sides, which is the right direction.

The honest limit: the maths assumes players are booked independently, and they are not — a flashpoint books two at once, and a referee losing control books five. That makes the extreme tails slightly thin, so treat O5.5 and above as a floor rather than a price. Fixtures with fewer than twelve rated players are marked thin sample.

Home and away

Away sides collect more cards than home sides, in every season on record. Booking heat has reflected that for a while through each club's home/away cards-against split; now the per-player number does too — a player is priced at ×0.95 at home and ×1.08 away, the factors carried over from the forecast pipeline. Across the league that opens a gap of about 0.2 cards a match between the two sides, which is the size the match record shows.

Head to head — what these two actually do to each other

Where the two clubs have met at least three times in the last five seasons, the market strip carries an H2H cell: the average yellows across those meetings, with the number of games in the label and the recent run in the tooltip. It counts yellows only, exactly as the model's expected-cards figure does, so the two numbers are directly comparable. Reds are recorded separately rather than folded in.

It is deliberately kept beside the model rather than inside it. They are two independent reads of the same question — one from this season's player rates, one from what actually happened when these sides met — and the disagreement is the useful part. Across the 120 fixtures where both exist they differ by 0.61 cards on average, and by a full card or more in about one in five. Those are the fixtures worth a second look, so the H2H figure is highlighted when it diverges by a card or more.

Treat it as context, not proof. Squads turn over, managers change, and ten meetings is a small sample — a fixture with a violent history and none of the same players is a coincidence, not a signal. Pairs that have not met enough show nothing at all, which is most of the promoted clubs. Data is the public-domain football-data.co.uk match record (1,118 meetings across 151 club pairs), the same source behind the referee figures and the home/away splits, so it adds no new dependency.

Matchup context

Derby fixtures — the recognised rivalries among the 20 clubs, North London to Tyne–Wear, plus the promoted clubs' regional rivalries — get a booking-heat boost and a 🔥 derby chip on the Fixtures tab, since rivalry games run hotter with the referee. Once at least two finished gameweeks have been recorded, the players table adds a small ▲ card-form trend beside the yellow count: cards picked up over the recent recorded window (up to the last five gameweeks). Booking heat is also venue-aware: each club's cards-against is split into home and away rates (football-data.co.uk match data — away sides consistently pick up more cards), so the same two clubs read differently at each ground. Each player now carries both fouls committed and fouls won per 90 (shown on the player profile, in the share cards and the CSV) — committed feeds the risk score today; fouls won is a new data slot that populates on the next ScoutingStats refresh (it isn't in the live FPL feed), and will sharpen opponent-aggression matchup calls once it's in.

The match model (game state)

The desk models cards. It does not model football matches — so it borrowed one. Plsimulator fits a Dixon–Coles attack/defence model to three seasons of results each week and publishes the ratings; the desk vendors them and reproduces its arithmetic exactly, so both products quote the same match.

It is used for one thing on your numbers: game state. A side being outplayed chases the game and fouls tactically; a comfortable favourite does not. Each fixture card shows the model's win probabilities, and every player's booking chance is scaled by his own side's expected result share — the underdog's players up, the favourite's down, on the log-odds scale like every other factor here. In practice it runs between about ×0.89 and ×1.11 and is clamped at ×0.85–×1.20; a level fixture is exactly neutral, and the two sides' factors are mirror images, so a mismatch redistributes risk between the sides rather than adding any to the league.

Each card also shows tight: the fitted chance the match finishes within one goal. Cards follow games that stay live, so this is a better closeness signal than a fixed list of rivalries — but it is shown and not yet used. Swapping what orders the fixture list is a decision for a backtest, so booking heat still uses the derby list. Fixtures the model has not rated get no game-state factor at all rather than an assumed average, and the desk works exactly as it did before if the model file is missing.

Suspension watch

Premier League bans trigger at 5 yellows (up to gameweek 19), 10 (up to gameweek 32) and 15 (all season). The strip above the players table flags anyone one booking from a ban — a player with a card to burn is a lean, one protecting a threshold often isn't — and lists players currently suspended or injured so you don't back an absentee.

Where the numbers come from

Player and club form is 2025-26 from ScoutingStats, refreshed in season with live card counts from the official FPL API. Seventeen clubs are on Premier League data. At the three promoted clubs, Coventry, Ipswich and Hull, the players the Championship feed covered carry their 2025-26 form there, flagged EFL, since that is their only recent record; the rest of each squad comes from the free FPL feed and is flagged new, with no rate at all until 2026-27 minutes accrue. Referee figures are 2025-26 season averages from public data, with each official's long-run record (and the league's multi-decade card trend) from the MIT-licensed epldata package up to 2017/18 and the public-domain football-data.co.uk match records from 2018/19 onward — cautions only, so it contextualises rather than prices.

Known limits

  • Until the 2026-27 season starts, risk scores are last season's form as the pre-season basis. Live card rates take over per player at 450 minutes.
  • Fouls are not in the FPL feed, so the fouls half of the risk score stays on 2025-26 form all season.
  • Promoted clubs (Coventry, Hull, Ipswich) are flagged EFL throughout, and their team rate is not shown because the Championship data mixes cup minutes. Where a player does carry a Championship rate, treat it as indicative: it's a different and rougher division.
  • Only a handful of promoted-club players cleared the Championship feed's minutes floor. The rest of each squad is listed from the FPL feed with no card rate — flagged new, shown as dashes, ranked below anyone with evidence, and hidden by the low-sample tick box. That's deliberate: a squad with a missing centre-back is worse than one that admits it doesn't know yet, and a zero would rank him the calmest defender in the division.
  • Referee appointments are not in the FPL feed. Assign the official on each fixture card once appointments are published (usually a few days out) — the assignment saves to this browser and folds the referee's card rate into that fixture's booking heat, and watchlisted players get a strict-ref flag above the players table.
